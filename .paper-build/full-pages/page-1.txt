Abstract—This study examines whether annual climate 
variables and state identity can estimate crop gross state value 
added (GSVA) across India. A panel of 385 complete observations 
covers 28 states from financial year 2011 –12 to 2024 –25. Daily 
gridded rain fall and temperature from the India Meteorological 
Department are aligned with the Oceanic Niño Index and 
RBI/NSO crop GSVA at constant 2011–12 prices. Five regressors 
are trained on 280 observations through 2020 –21 and evaluated 
on 105 later observations.  Ordinary least squares produces the 
lowest test root mean squared error among the fitted models, at 
INR 17,410.41 crore, with R² = 0.8783. However, carrying forward 
each state's final training -year GSVA reduces the error to INR 
10,890.73 crore. Permuting climate inputs produces small changes 
in linear -model error, suggesting limited additional predictive 
information in this specification. A working web application 
exposes the harmonized panel, model comparisons, predictions, 
and conditional scenarios. The results establish a reproducible 
state-level assessment while showing that strong pooled fit does not 
establish reliable climate attribution or improvement over a simple 
persistence benchmark. 
Keywords—El Niño, Indian agriculture, crop GSVA, panel 
data, machine learning, chronological evaluation 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Climate variability affects agricultural production 
through water availability, temperature exposure, and the 
timing of crop development. Evidence from India links 
agricultural outcomes to seasonal climate, but the 
relationship depends on the crop, region,  and period studied 
[1]. The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) adds a large-
scale source of variation: Pacific sea -surface temperature 
anomalies are associated with changes in Indian rainfall [2]. 
This association has varied over time [3], and the location and 
evolution of Pacific warming matter for interpreting monsoon 
failure [4]. A state -level economic application therefore 
needs to test its own predictive performance rather than 
assume that an ENSO signal automatically translates into 
accurate estimates of crop value added. 
The Indian monsoon contains substantial spatial and 
temporal variability [5]. Indian Ocean conditions can modify 
the monsoon–ENSO association [6], while the Indian Ocean 
Dipole provides a distinct source of climate variability [7]. 
Different Pacific warmin g patterns also have different 
teleconnections [8]. These findings motivate the inclusion of 
local rainfall and temperature alongside an ENSO index. 
They also caution against assigning every low-output year to 
El Niño. In the present model, rainfall, temperature, and the 
Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) are explanatory inputs to a 
prediction system; none is treated as an experimentally 
isolated cause. 
The economic outcome considered here is crop gross state 
value added, expressed in INR crore at constant 2011 –12 
prices. This measure differs from crop yield, farm profit, 
household income, and the value of all agriculture and allied 
activities. It provide s a common monetary scale for 
comparing state observations, while retaining large 
differences in the size and composition of their crop sectors. 
These differences make state identity useful for pooled 
prediction, but they also create a risk: a model can ob tain a 
high R² by separating large and small state economies without 
accurately tracking changes within a state. 
This study addresses three questions. First, how do a 
linear model and four tree ensembles compare on a later -
period holdout? Second, do these models improve on state -
specific historical benchmarks? Third, how much does the 
fitted linear model depend on th e climate inputs when state 
identity is held fixed? The work combines official -data 
harmonization, a reproducible comparison of saved models, 
and an application that makes the results inspectable. The 
central finding is that the linear model leads the five  fitted 
regressors, yet a simple last -observed-value benchmark 
performs substantially better. This result determines the 
limits placed on the application's prediction and scenario 
outputs. 
II. RELATED WORK 
A. ENSO and Agricultural Outcomes 
Research on ENSO and the monsoon supplies physical 
context, but the choice of index remains consequential. Work 
on ENSO indices in a warming climate shows why index 
definitions and background warming require attention [9]. 
The present analysis retains the historical ONI series used by 
the implemented pipeline. It does not substitute a relative 
index after model fitting or equate an annual ONI average 
with a categorical declaration of an El Niño episode. Such a 
substitution would alter the inputs and require a new training 
and evaluation cycle.
