Economic research using weather variation distinguishes 
statistical association from the identification of an economic 
response [14]. The distinction is especially relevant when 
output trends, irrigation, crop choice, and technology are 
incompletely observ ed. The goal here is a transparent 
comparison of predictive specifications. The fitted 
relationships are not interpreted as causal damage functions, 
and a model -generated difference between two weather 
inputs is not presented as an observed economic loss. 
B. Drought Vulnerability and Spatial Assessment 
Drought research distinguishes meteorological deficits 
from agricultural and socioeconomic consequences [15]. 
Agricultural vulnerability assessments consider the systems 
exposed to a hazard, including their sensitivity and capacity 
to respond [16]. Survey evidence from Maharashtra 
documents farmers' experiences of drought impacts and 
adaptation [17]. District -scale studies further examine how 
exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity vary across 
Maharashtra [18]. This literature explains why rainfall alo ne 
is an incomplete indicator of agricultural harm. 
Several Indian studies combine indicators into spatial 
vulnerability assessments. Analytical hierarchy methods 
have been applied to disaster vulnerability [19] and, in fuzzy 
form, to drought vulnerability in Odisha [20]. Composite 
assessments have also bee n developed for Tamil Nadu [21], 
agricultural drought mapping [22], and hazard -specific 
evaluation under the IPCC framework [23]. A study of 
Vidarbha provides a regional example of drought 
vulnerability assessment [24]. These approaches address 
relative vu lnerability rather than prediction of a monetary 
outcome. Their mapped categories should therefore not be 
treated as labels for the regression task used in this paper. 
The broader conceptual literature emphasizes that 
vulnerability depends on the system, stressor, and assessment 
objective [25], [26], [27]. Principal component analysis has 
supported information -rich vulnerability mapping [28], but 
its role is to summarize  variation in the input matrix [29]. 
Research on composite indicators also emphasizes sensitivity 
analysis when assessing the reliability of rankings [30]. This 
project does not compute a PCA vulnerability index. It retains 
the useful emphasis on traceable  inputs and clear 
interpretation while evaluating predicted crop GSVA directly 
against held-out economic observations. 
C. Machine Learning and Evaluation 
Reviews of agricultural machine learning document 
varied tasks, input types, and modeling approaches [31], [32]. 
The present comparison uses established ensemble families: 
Random Forest [33], Extra Trees [34], gradient boosting [35], 
and XGBoost [36]. Thei r flexibility permits interactions and 
nonlinear splits, but the relatively small panel does not ensure 
that this flexibility improves performance. Ordinary least 
squares is therefore included as a transparent comparator. 
Time-structured data require evaluation designs that 
respect their dependence structure [37], [38]. Error estimates 
can also become optimistic when model selection and 
performance assessment use the same data repeatedly [39]. 
Accordingly, the reported spli t places earlier financial years 
in training and later years in testing. The comparison uses the 
saved configurations, without claiming an exhaustive 
hyperparameter search. The holdout establishes a 
retrospective comparison for known states; it does not 
establish transfer to unseen states, future ENSO events, or a 
real-time forecast made before weather observations are 
available. 
III. STUDY AREA AND DATA 
A. Spatial and Temporal Scope 
The study covers 28 Indian states over 14 financial years, 
from 2011–12 through 2024–25. A financial year is identified 
by its starting calendar year and runs from April to March. 
The candidate panel therefore contains 392 state -year 
combinations. Seven la ck an economic target, leaving 385 
complete observations. Union territories are outside the 
model's state vocabulary. The panel includes diverse crop 
economies, but each state is represented by a single weather 
sampling location rather than an area -weighted climate 
surface. 
The excluded economic observations are Goa for 2024 –
25, Gujarat for 2023 –24 and 2024 –25, and Manipur, 
Mizoram, Nagaland, and Sikkim for 2024–25. Missing labels 
are not filled with model predictions or assumed zeros. 
Consequently, the final two test years h ave fewer states than 
the earlier test years. The coverage pattern is shown in Fig. 2 
and must be considered when comparing annual errors: 
changes in the evaluated state mix accompany changes in the 
year. 
The panel is balanced during training, with ten 
observations for every state. Its incompleteness is 
concentrated in the evaluation period: the test set contains 
105 of 112 possible state -years. This distinction matters 
because the fitted state indicators a re learned from equal 
numbers of training years, while pooled test metrics weight 
states according to their available observations. Gujarat 
contributes two test rows and five other states contribute 
three, compared with four for the remaining states. No 
weighting correction is applied to make these contributions 
equal. The reported metrics therefore describe the observed 
complete-case holdout, with its actual coverage. 
B. Climate and Economic Sources 
Daily rainfall is taken from the IMD 0.25° gridded 
product, whose development is documented by Pai et al. [40]. 
Daily maximum and minimum temperatures use the IMD 1° 
gridded product, with the gridded -temperature methodology 
described by Srivastava et al. [41]. For each state, the pipeline 
selects the nearest cell to a configured representative 
coordinate. Rainfall and temperature can therefore use 
different grid -cell centers. Their coordinates and valid -day 
counts remain in the prepared panel for audit.
